### 3. Educational Section: "Why You Need a Textile Production Designer (Not Just Any Graphic Designer)"
This is the most important section. Write genuinely educational content in plain language that a textile brand manager, sourcing manager, or product developer would find valuable. Cover:

**A. The Substrate Problem — Why the Same Color Looks Different on Cotton, Polyester, and Silk**
Explain that different fibers absorb dye differently due to their molecular structure (amorphous vs crystalline regions), pH sensitivity, and surface reflectance. Include concrete examples:
- On the same dyeing condition, wool can produce a much deeper color depth (K/S = 4.4) than silk (K/S = 1.42) or cotton (K/S = 0.93) because wool has more amorphous regions [citation:7]
- Polyester dyed at 130°C can actually produce *lower* color depth than at 100°C because the dye decomposes at higher temperatures [citation:7]
- The same Pantone reference applied to different fabrics will not visually match — this is physics, not a defect [citation:6]

**B. What Delta-E Means and Why It Costs You Money**
Explain in accessible terms:
- Delta-E (ΔE) is the numerical measurement of color difference between a target and what actually comes out of production [citation:5]
- The textile industry typically requires ΔE ≤ 1.5 or even ≤ 0.5 for premium work
- A ΔE of 3 is often the maximum acceptable in some commercial standards, but for high-end brands this is far too loose
- Explain what happens when Delta-E is ignored: fabric gets rejected, seasons get missed, brand reputation suffers, and money is wasted on strike-offs that never get approved [citation:11]

**C. The Pantone TCX Difference — Why Paper Swatches Are Not Enough**
- TCX means "Textile Cotton eXtended" — engineered cotton swatches designed for accurate textile matching [citation:6]
- TPX/TPG are paper versions, and only about 60-80% of them match the cotton TCX standard well [citation:6]
- Dark and saturated colors (black, navy, deep reds) rarely match on paper because it's difficult to build sufficient depth of color on paper [citation:6]
- If a brand specifies color from a paper guide, the mill may produce something that is technically "correct" but visually wrong [citation:20]

**D. What Happens Without a Production Designer**
Explain the chain of consequences:
- Lab dips fail repeatedly, adding weeks of delay
- Dye lots drift across large orders, producing visible color variations in the final product [citation:11]
- 1-5% extra fabric is routinely ordered just to compensate for color and defect issues [citation:11]
- The brand's reputation takes a hit when customers receive products that don't match what they saw on screen or in a showroom
